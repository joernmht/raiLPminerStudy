Disruptions on the railway network can lead to reduced availability of the railway infrastructure, which requires rolling stock dispatchers to adjust the planning of the rolling stock. In this paper, we develop fast rolling stock rescheduling methods which ensure feasibility with respect to the availability of the railway infrastructure. In particular, we explore the option of performing shunting movements at stations where shunting does not take place in current practice, due to the large number of trains that pass through or due to the complexity of the station layout. We introduce an exact rolling stock rescheduling algorithm and an iterative heuristic, which alternate between two mathematical formulations, namely one that creates an interim rolling stock schedule and one that tries to fit the suggested shunting movements between the remaining railway traffic. We test our solution approach with instances that contain complete railway blockages on the Dutch railway network. We successfully identify feasible shunting movements and find an average improvement in the objective function of 19% over the rolling stock schedule that would be obtained if performing shunting movements at the considered stations is prohibited.

Introduction

With millions of passenger trips on an average day, railway networks play a big role in the European mobility system. In the European Union, the railway infrastructure and the railway operations are mandated by law to be managed separately. In the Netherlands, ProRail is responsible for managing the railway network infrastructure and Netherlands Railways (NS) is the main passenger railway operator. One of the tasks of the railway operator is the planning of individual train units, or rolling stock. A rolling stock schedule specifies which rolling stock units operate which trips in the timetable. Disruptions on the railway network, such as a missing rolling stock unit in a train trip or a complete blockage of a railway track as a result of an accident or a train breakdown, require the rolling stock plan to be updated. The rescheduling measures that pertain to the (availability of the) railway infrastructure, i.e., the changes in the rolling stock assignments, are created by rolling stock dispatchers who work for the railway operator, after which local infrastructure dispatchers who work for the infrastructure manager are tasked with performing the suggested rolling stock movements.

We focus our research on the rescheduling of rolling stock units in the case of disruptions in the railway system. It is necessary that the rolling stock is efficiently operated and utilized to maintain a good balance between the service to passengers and the incurred operational costs. Unavailability of rolling stock influences passenger satisfaction, as passengers possibly have to stand or even wait for the next train in case not enough capacity is available. An efficient rescheduling process reduces such problems and also reduces the number of trains that are needed as a buffer to catch disrupted train services, leading to a reduction in the capital costs for the train operator. It is also beneficial if the adjusted plan is similar to the original plan, as this requires fewer rolling stock movements during the night to get each rolling stock unit to its starting position for the next day. Additionally, an advantage for both passengers and crew members is that they are familiar with the original plan and may prefer the rolling stock units to be operated as originally planned.

Currently, the rescheduling of rolling stock after disruptions is mostly done by hand. As a result, railway planners manually try to find an updated rolling stock allocation without the use of any mathematical methods or solvers. This can largely be attributed to the difficulty of the problem. For a rolling stock plan to work in practice, feasible train compositions need to be assigned to each train trip, the associated shunting movements need to be executable and shunting drivers need to be available for all shunting movements. Past papers have often tackled these problems separately, meaning that there are practical requirements that are not considered in their solution approaches. One of these requirements is the availability of railway tracks for the shunting movements to take place. To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has focused on ensuring feasibility of a rolling stock schedule with regard to this aspect. In practice, this is an important part of rolling stock rescheduling; if the rolling stock movements are not possible due to unavailability of the railway tracks, the entire plan could fall apart and could need to be adjusted.

We introduce two solution approaches which ensure that all shunting movements are feasible with respect to the available infrastructure: an exact algorithm and an iterative heuristic. The basis for our methods is the composition model by Fioole et al. (2006), which outputs a rolling stock circulation for the passenger train timetable. To check whether each of the suggested trips and shunting movements can be performed, we implement an infrastructure model which is based on the model by Van Aken et al. (2017) for solving the Train Timetable Adjustment Problem, which aims at finding an alternative timetable that minimizes the deviation from the original timetable in terms of the incurred delays and cancellations of train services. Our tailored version of this model represents every shunting movement as an event that takes place on a specific track at a specific station and outputs whether a shunting movement is possible and whether other trains need to be delayed to make the shunting movement possible. Although delaying other trains is typically not preferred, a short delay could open up the necessary windows for performing additional shunting movements. We therefore include the option of delaying other trains by at most a few minutes in our framework and assume that such short delays are resolved by the buffers that are present in the timetable for the remainder of the affected train lines.

Our exact approach resembles a Combinatorial Benders’ approach in line with Codato and Fischetti (2004). It alternates between the composition model, which suggests shunting movements, and the infrastructure model, which checks whether each combination of shunting movements can be performed. The infrastructure model then returns cuts that prohibit combinations of shunting movements, which violate the railway infrastructure availability constraints, from being performed simultaneously. A drawback of the exact approach is the underlying assumption that the creation of a new rolling stock plan and the performance of the shunting movements are carried out simultaneously by one and the same entity, which conflicts with the separation of the railway tasks as mandated by European laws. We therefore also introduce an iterative heuristic, which works in a similar way as the exact algorithm, but feeds the suggested shunting movements one by one to the infrastructure model. This closely resembles the situation where the rolling stock dispatchers and infrastructure dispatchers work for different companies and is therefore more applicable in the European context. The iterative heuristic mimics the interaction that takes place between the rolling stock dispatchers, who try to come up with a new rolling stock plan, and the local infrastructure dispatchers, who try to perform the shunting movements. We want to emphasize that our framework is generic; besides the specific model choices that we made for the two steps in our iterative frameworks, other formulations and methods can be used as well, so long as they solve the same problems.

We test our algorithm on seven instances which consist of full blockages on the railway network. We model and analyze the railway infrastructure of two major stations, Utrecht Centraal and Rotterdam Centraal, and allow for shunting movements at these two stations during the duration of the blockages. In practice, shunting at these two stations is assumed to be too difficult due to the large number of trains that pass through and is therefore not considered a viable option during the creation of the rolling stock schedule. We show that the exact algorithm successfully identifies feasible shunting movements that can take place at both Utrecht Centraal and Rotterdam Centraal to counter the disruption and to improve the rolling stock schedule with respect to several operational, passenger service level and stick-to-the-plan objectives compared to the situation in which shunting at Utrecht Centraal and Rotterdam Centraal is not considered. Additionally, we find that the iterative heuristic is able to obtain an optimal solution for all instances, in shorter running times. We find that shunting movements are only canceled in case other passenger trains occupy the railway infrastructure and that the shunting movements themselves do not create unsolvable conflicts with one another. Therefore, the order in which the shunting movements are processed by the infrastructure model does not matter and the iterative heuristic finds an optimal solution for all instances. We furthermore find that our algorithm performs well in case we put emphasis on any of the rolling stock rescheduling objectives and that our approach also provides added value in the case that all trains are fixed and cannot be delayed.

The contribution of this paper is threefold. Firstly, we introduce frameworks for rolling stock rescheduling with railway infrastructure availability constraints. The methods output rolling stock circulations which ensure that a feasible path through the railway infrastructure exists for the performed shunting movements at the considered stations. Secondly, our algorithms find solutions within a few minutes with an average improvement of 19% over the rolling stock schedule which does not contain shunting movements at Utrecht Centraal or Rotterdam Centraal. Thirdly, we show that the frameworks can be beneficial in practical problems; by allowing for shunting at more stations than is currently done in practice, our frameworks are able to provide improvements over the rolling stock circulations that are currently made. Furthermore, the iterative approach mimics rolling stock rescheduling in practice by distinguishing between the two problems which are currently solved separately by the rolling stock dispatchers and the infrastructure dispatchers. Our approach provides a framework which connects these problems, thereby facilitating and reducing the interaction which is required between the involved parties.
