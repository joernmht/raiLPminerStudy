The real-time railway traffic management problem occurs when the pre-planned timetable cannot be kept due to various disturbances; therefore, the trains must be rerouted, reordered, and rescheduled. Optimizing the real-time railway traffic management aims to resolve conflicts, minimizing the delay propagation or energy consumption. In one of our previous works, the existing mixed-integer linear programming optimization models are extended considering a safety-relevant issue of railway traffic management, the overlaps. However, solving the extended model can be time-consuming in complex control areas and traffic situations involving numerous trains. Therefore, we propose different computationally efficient multi-stage models by decomposing the problem according to the rerouting, reordering, and rescheduling sub-problems. First, a lightweight heuristic MILP model that provides a fast but sub-optimal solution is given by reformulating the train delays into pair-wise interpretation. Second, we extend the heuristic model to grant an optimal solution to the original problem faster than the existing MILP formulations. The impact of the model decomposition is investigated mathematically and experimentally in various realistic simulated traffic scenarios concerning the optimization’s objective value and computational demand. The proposed multi-stage models significantly decrease the optimization runtime of both the original and the extended railway traffic management problems.

Introduction

The considerable potential for reducing CO2 emissions lies in the low specific energy consumption of rail transportation in comparison to other sectors (García-Olivares et al., 2018; Dalla Chiara et al., 2017). The climate protection initiatives and increasing energy prices will encourage the railway to play a more significant role in future transport, increasing the demand for passenger and freight rail services (Chaturvedi and Kim, 2015; Islam et al., 2015; Boldizsár, 2024). Moreover, increasing infrastructure utilization and decreasing energy consumption and carbon emission is one of the most crucial research areas in railway traffic (González-Gil et al., 2014; Li and Zhang, 2020). The timetable optimization is one of the key components of increasing energy efficiency (Yang et al., 2016; Corlu et al.). However, in many cases, the optimally scheduled trains suffer primary delays due to different traffic disturbances, and the pre-planned timetable would result in conflicting trains (Nagy and Csiszár, 2015). Therefore, the trains must be rescheduled, rerouted, or reordered, assigning secondary delays to the trains to solve the real-time railway traffic management problem (rtRTMP) (Corman, 2010). Nowadays, generally, human dispatchers resolve conflicts according to several generic and regional principles, such as the first-in-first-out (FIFO) policy. However, making an optimal decision considering different objectives and traffic regulations within the required short response time is very challenging for a human dispatcher. Hence, automatic railway traffic management and control have become a popular research topic in recent years, aiming to reduce energy consumption and optimize capacity usage to satisfy the increasing demands (Pellegrini et al., 2016; Botte and D’Acierno, 2018).

Related works

Many works tackle the real-time railway traffic management problem with different optimization approaches and models (Corman and Meng, 2015; Cacchiani et al., 2014; Fang et al., 2015), tackling different uncertainties (Zhan et al., 2024). The models can be divided into microscopic and macroscopic groups according to the levels of the infrastructure representation (Cacchiani et al., 2014; Qu et al., 2015). Microscopic models describe the topology of the rail network, including detailed information such as track length and velocity, as in Rodriguez (2007) and D’Ariano et al. (2008). Macroscopic models neglect some lower-level resources such as signals, reducing the complexity of the infrastructure model (Dollevoet et al., 2015; Kanai et al., 2011). The models can also be distinguished according to the objective function. Usually, the optimization goal is to minimize train delays, as in the proposed method. Some works also consider the energy consumption after determining the routing and ordering that minimizes the delays, as in Naldini et al. (2022, 2021), Montrone et al. (2018), or a trade-off is made between them (Rao et al., 2013; D’Ariano et al., 2010; ShangGuan et al., 2015).

The optimization models of the rtRTMP also range widely, but mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) is one of the most common formulations, as in Acuna-Agost et al. (2011), Pellegrini et al. (2012, 2014), Törnquist Krasemann (2015). However, since the problem is NP-hard, in the case of large networks and complex scenarios, the runtime of the optimization can exceed the available response time, particularly in large, complex networks. Therefore, a runtime-efficient implementation is essential in a real traffic environment. Some custom heuristics and additional constraints are introduced in Boccia et al. (2013), Pellegrini et al. (2015) to decrease the computational time of the MILP. Furthermore, Pellegrini et al. (2019) proposed an efficient implementation by extending their previous work (Pellegrini et al., 2015) with new constraints, decreasing the number of equivalent combinations of integer variables. Another approach to reducing the model’s complexity is decomposing it according to smaller sub-problems. The number of routing alternatives strongly affects the time needed to find the global optimum. Therefore, it is reasonable to solve the route selection problem first, providing a smaller subset of routing alternatives for the MILP model, as in Bettinelli et al. (2017), Sama et al. (2017), Gholami and Törnquist Krasemann (2018). The train route selection problem (TRSP) was first formalized by Samà et al. (2016) with a construction graph along with an ant colony optimization (ACO) algorithm to solve it. The main idea was to select the routes that minimize the pair-wise delay of trains, neglecting the delay propagation. Pascariu et al. (2022) extended the TRSP model with an estimation of delay propagation. For the case of complete blockage, Zhan et al. (2024) also propose a two-stage approach, but the problem is decomposed according to disruption phases, i.e., during and after disruption, instead of the rerouting and rescheduling sub-problems. Benders decomposition (BD) (BnnoBRs, 1962) is an alternative strategy that provides the optimal solution by iteratively solving the master and a set of sub-problems. For example, the line traffic and station control problems are Benders decomposed in Mannino (2011), Lamorgese and Mannino (2013, 2015), Lamorgese et al. (2016) that shows a good performance in large networks. Instead of spatial considerations, the rtRTMP is decomposed according to rerouting and rescheduling problems using BD in Keita et al. (2016). Moreover, a three-step decomposition, considering the rerouting, reordering, and rescheduling problems, is presented in Keita et al. (2020) similarly to the proposed method but in a Benders’ manner. Meng and Zhou (2014) also propose a MILP formulation based on cumulative flow variables, allowing solving the rerouting and rescheduling problems simultaneously. Besides the decomposition of MILP models, other methods focusing on runtime-efficient dispatching also exist. For example, Corman et al. (2010) formulate the problem as an alternative graph model, incorporating effective rescheduling algorithms and local rerouting strategies in a tabu search scheme. Meanwhile, Törnquist (Törnquist Krasemann, 2012) proposes an efficient greedy approach based on depth-first search, providing a solution within seconds.

Contributions of the paper

This paper proposes a multi-stage framework consisting of two different MILP models, minimizing the overall delays by decomposing the rtRTMP into rerouting, reordering, and rescheduling sub-problems, similar to Keita et al. (2020). However, to avoid multiple iterations, the proposed method does not rely on Benders’ algorithm. First, a low-complex, simplified approach is presented that solves the rerouting and reordering, providing a fast but sub-optimal solution for the original rtRTMP in Pellegrini et al. (2014). It relies on reforming the standard delay variables (e.g., in Pellegrini et al. (2014, 2015, 2019)) into a pair-wise interpretation (i.e., indicating which other train they are waiting for) while disregarding delay propagation. Then, to tackle rescheduling, the original problem is solved with the fixed route selection and optional train ordering result provided by this sub-optimal model. Similar considerations are taken in Samà et al. (2016); however, we propose it in a MILP formulation that provides an exact route selection that can further decrease the complexity of the optimization. Furthermore, we analyzed the optimality condition to obtain the circumstance in which this approach results in a sub-optimal solution. As a result of the analysis, we developed a new model that guarantees the global optimum for the original rtRTMP in Pellegrini et al. (2014) by extending the sub-optimal model to consider the delay propagation. This formulation of the rtRTMP shows a more favorable computational cost compared to the original in Pellegrini et al. (2014), especially in complex scenarios. Finally, both models provide an input route selection and optionally reordering for the extended rtRTMP in Lindenmaier et al. (2022), considering the safety-relevant overlaps, which are the margins that have to be proved clear behind a stop signal.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: the existing reference MILP models are introduced in Section 2. The proposed sub-optimal solution is detailed in Section 3.1. The optimality condition of the first approach is analyzed in Section 3.2, and the resulting global optimum model is explained in Section 3.3. The results, along with the experimental setup, are discussed in Section 4. Finally, Section 5 outlines the conclusions along with a brief outlook on future works.
